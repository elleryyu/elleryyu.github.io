I never thought I'd spend a Sunday afternoon watching an AI agent clean my inbox, update my personal website, and answer my questions about Overleaf — all through a Telegram chat. But here we are.

This is a short reflection on why I've started to genuinely embrace **agentic AI**, and what it's changed about how I work.

---

## From Chatbot to Agent

Most people's experience with AI still looks like this: you type a question, you get an answer, you copy-paste something useful, you move on. It's powerful, but it's still *you* doing the work. The AI is a consultant, not a colleague.

Agentic AI is different. An agent doesn't just answer — it **acts**. It can read your files, run commands, call APIs, send messages, and chain together multi-step tasks without you orchestrating every move. The shift feels small until you experience it, and then it feels enormous.

---

## What Changed for Me

I'm a CS graduate researcher. My days involve juggling papers, code, emails, writing, and an endless backlog of "I should update that sometime." The cognitive overhead of switching between tools — Gmail, GitHub, Overleaf, my website — adds up in ways that are hard to quantify but easy to feel.

When I started using [OpenClaw](https://github.com/openclaw/openclaw) with an agent configured around my workflow, things shifted. Not dramatically overnight, but gradually and then all at once.

Some concrete examples from a single session:

- I asked my agent to **archive every non-payment email** from my Gmail inbox. It listed what it found, confirmed the plan, and moved 44 emails to archive in one command. What would have taken me 20 minutes of scrolling took about 90 seconds.
- It **updated my personal website** — fixing a stale publication status, improving my bio, removing leftover template pages, adding Google Scholar and LinkedIn links, and writing a News section — all from a conversation in Telegram.
- When I asked about connecting Overleaf, it looked up the docs, explained the constraints honestly, and told me it couldn't do it without a paid plan rather than pretending otherwise.

That last point matters more than it sounds.

---

## Trust, Not Blind Faith

The thing I've come to appreciate about working with a well-designed agent is that **trust is earned incrementally**. Good agents confirm before deleting. They show you what they're about to do. They fail gracefully and tell you why.

I don't think of my agent as infallible. I think of it the way I think of a good research assistant: capable, fast, honest about limitations, and worth checking on for anything high-stakes.

The relationship works because I stay in the loop — not for every command, but for anything consequential. That feels right.

---

## The Bigger Picture

I study computational neuroimaging. I build models that try to decode how the brain organizes information across regions and timescales. In some ways, watching agentic AI evolve feels familiar: it's the same story of distributed systems developing the capacity to coordinate, act, and adapt.

What's new is that the agents are now operating in *my* environment — my inbox, my GitHub, my files. The boundary between "tool I use" and "collaborator I work with" is blurring in interesting ways.

I don't think this is something to fear. I think it's something to engage with carefully, with clear values about what you want agents to do and what you want to remain in your own hands.

For me, that line is roughly: agents handle the repetitive, the administrative, the connective tissue of digital work. The ideas, the research questions, the writing that actually matters — that stays mine.
