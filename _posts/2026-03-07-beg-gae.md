This post gives an overview of **BEG-GAE** (Behavioral-Enriched Graph Autoencoder), a framework I developed with collaborators to jointly model neuroimaging and behavioral data for understanding adolescent psychiatric disorders. The work was presented at **MIDL 2025**.

---

## Motivation

Adolescent psychiatric disorders — including ADHD, anxiety, and mood dysregulation — are among the most prevalent and least understood conditions in neuroscience. Neuroimaging studies have identified disrupted functional connectivity patterns in affected youth, but two persistent challenges limit progress:

1. **Unimodal analysis**: Most methods use brain connectivity *or* behavioral/cognitive scores — rarely both in an integrated, principled way.
2. **Individual variability**: The same diagnostic label often masks heterogeneous underlying neurobiology. Population-level statistics miss the structure within groups.

BEG-GAE addresses both challenges by treating each subject as a node in a graph, learning jointly from their brain connectivity and behavioral profiles.

---

## Framework Overview

BEG-GAE operates on **two graphs simultaneously**, each capturing a different level of brain-behavior organization.

### Graph 1: Brain Graph (FC-based)

The first graph represents the **individual brain connectivity network**. Brain regions (ROIs) are nodes, and edges are derived from **functional connectivity (FC)** — the pairwise Pearson correlations between regional BOLD time series. Each subject has their own brain graph, encoding their unique pattern of large-scale brain organization.

This graph is the primary input to the autoencoder and the object being reconstructed.

### Graph 2: Subject-to-Subject Behavioral Graph

The second graph operates at the **population level**. Each subject is a node, and edges are defined by the **cosine similarity of behavioral score vectors** — capturing how similar two subjects are in terms of cognitive and clinical profiles.

This graph encodes who-is-similar-to-whom in behavioral space, and serves as the relational structure through which the GCN propagates information across subjects.

### Graph Autoencoder

We adopt a **Graph Autoencoder (GAE)** architecture that operates on the brain graph:
- An **encoder** (Graph Convolutional Network) aggregates neighborhood information within the brain graph, producing a low-dimensional latent embedding per subject — guided by the subject-to-subject behavioral graph
- A **decoder** reconstructs the original FC matrix from the latent code

The key insight: by propagating information through the behavioral similarity graph, each subject's brain representation is *enriched* by peers with similar behavioral profiles — a principled form of cross-subject regularization.

---

## Training Objective

The core training objective is **unsupervised reconstruction**:

$$\mathcal{L} = \mathcal{L}_{\text{recon}}$$

where $\mathcal{L}_{\text{recon}}$ is the reconstruction loss on functional connectivity (MSE). The autoencoder learns low-dimensional latent representations by recovering the original connectivity structure from the graph-enriched embeddings — no labels required.

A classification loss ($\mathcal{L}_{\text{cls}}$) is optionally added **only when the downstream task involves biomarker identification or disorder classification**:

$$\mathcal{L} = \mathcal{L}_{\text{recon}} + \lambda \mathcal{L}_{\text{cls}}$$

This keeps the framework flexible: the GAE can be used purely for representation learning and connectivity reconstruction, and the supervised signal is introduced only when diagnostic prediction is the explicit goal.

---

## Key Results

Experiments on the **ABCD Study** cohort:

- BEG-GAE outperformed unimodal baselines (connectivity-only GCN, behavioral-only MLP) on disorder classification
- Latent embeddings revealed clinically meaningful clustering — subjects with similar behavioral profiles and connectivity patterns grouped together, even without explicit clustering supervision
- Ablation studies confirmed that both the behavioral graph construction and behavioral node augmentation contributed independently to performance gains

---

## What This Means

The framework makes a case for a specific design principle: **behavioral context should not be an afterthought in neuroimaging models**. When we treat behavior as a structural prior that organizes the subject graph, the model learns more generalizable brain-behavior relationships.

More broadly, this work fits into a growing literature on **population graphs** and **multi-modal GNNs** in medical imaging — a direction I find increasingly compelling as the field moves from voxel-level analysis toward system-level, subject-level modeling.
