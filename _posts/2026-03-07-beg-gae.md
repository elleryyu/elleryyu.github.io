This post gives an overview of **BEG-GAE** (Behavioral-Enriched Graph Autoencoder), a framework I developed with collaborators to jointly model neuroimaging and behavioral data for understanding adolescent psychiatric disorders. The work was presented at **MIDL 2025**.

---

## Motivation

Adolescent psychiatric disorders — including ADHD, anxiety, and mood dysregulation — are among the most prevalent and least understood conditions in neuroscience. Neuroimaging studies have identified disrupted functional connectivity patterns in affected youth, but two persistent challenges limit progress:

1. **Unimodal analysis**: Most methods use brain connectivity *or* behavioral/cognitive scores — rarely both in an integrated, principled way.
2. **Individual variability**: The same diagnostic label often masks heterogeneous underlying neurobiology. Population-level statistics miss the structure within groups.

BEG-GAE addresses both challenges by treating each subject as a node in a graph, learning jointly from their brain connectivity and behavioral profiles.

---

## Framework Overview

### Subject Graph Construction

Rather than analyzing brain connectivity matrices in isolation, BEG-GAE constructs a **subject-level graph**: each subject is a node, and edges encode similarity between subjects based on their behavioral/demographic features (cognitive scores, diagnostic measures, age, sex).

This is inspired by population graph approaches in medical imaging, but extended to incorporate richer behavioral encodings.

### Node Features: Brain Connectivity

Each node (subject) carries a feature vector derived from **fMRI functional connectivity** — either the upper triangle of the ROI-to-ROI correlation matrix, or a lower-dimensional embedding from a pretrained encoder.

### Graph Autoencoder

We adopt a **Graph Autoencoder (GAE)** architecture:
- An **encoder** (Graph Convolutional Network) aggregates information from each subject's neighborhood, producing a low-dimensional latent embedding
- A **decoder** reconstructs the original connectivity matrix from the latent code

The key insight: by propagating information through the behavioral similarity graph, each subject's representation is *enriched* by similar peers — a form of graph-based regularization that improves both reconstruction and downstream classification.

### Behavioral Enrichment

The "B" in BEG-GAE refers to how behavioral features are integrated at *two* levels:
1. **Graph construction** — behavioral similarity defines the topology of who-is-connected-to-whom
2. **Node augmentation** — behavioral features are concatenated with connectivity features as input to the GCN encoder

This dual integration ensures behavioral context shapes both the structure and the content of the learned representations.

---

## Training Objective

The model is trained with a combined loss:

$$\mathcal{L} = \mathcal{L}_{\text{recon}} + \lambda \mathcal{L}_{\text{cls}}$$

where:
- $\mathcal{L}_{\text{recon}}$ is the reconstruction loss on functional connectivity (MSE)
- $\mathcal{L}_{\text{cls}}$ is a cross-entropy loss for disorder classification using the latent embeddings
- $\lambda$ balances the two objectives

This semi-supervised formulation allows the model to learn representations that are both neurobiologically faithful (good reconstruction) and diagnostically informative (predictive of disorder).

---

## Key Results

Experiments on the **ABCD Study** cohort:

- BEG-GAE outperformed unimodal baselines (connectivity-only GCN, behavioral-only MLP) on disorder classification
- Latent embeddings revealed clinically meaningful clustering — subjects with similar behavioral profiles and connectivity patterns grouped together, even without explicit clustering supervision
- Ablation studies confirmed that both the behavioral graph construction and behavioral node augmentation contributed independently to performance gains

---

## What This Means

The framework makes a case for a specific design principle: **behavioral context should not be an afterthought in neuroimaging models**. When we treat behavior as a structural prior that organizes the subject graph, the model learns more generalizable brain-behavior relationships.

More broadly, this work fits into a growing literature on **population graphs** and **multi-modal GNNs** in medical imaging — a direction I find increasingly compelling as the field moves from voxel-level analysis toward system-level, subject-level modeling.
